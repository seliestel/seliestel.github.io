# For instructors

This companion is designed to make *Into Semiotics* teachable in an active, student-centered way, in line with the book's own pedagogical aims. Everything here is optional and modular: pick the activities that suit your syllabus, your class size, and your students' backgrounds.

## How each activity is structured

Every activity carries the same lightweight metadata so you can plan at a glance:

- **Type** — Individual, Pair, Group, Whole-class, or Fieldwork.
- **Time** — a rough estimate for the core task (excludes any homework collection).
- **Tech** — flags activities that use phones, video, or online tools (the reviewer's "technology-based interactive activities").
- **Linked book sections** — the exact sections the activity practises, so you can assign reading first.
- **Instructor notes** — a collapsible box with an answer key, model interpretations, common student errors, and discussion prompts.

## Suggested pacing

A typical 15-week postgraduate seminar maps cleanly onto the book:

| Weeks | Book chapters | Companion focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Ch 1–2 | Sign safari; morning-in-signs; historical timeline |
| 3–4 | Ch 3 | Signifier/signified; arbitrariness; value & difference |
| 5–6 | Ch 4 | Icon/index/symbol sorting; interpretant chains |
| 7–8 | Ch 5 | Jakobson's functions; poetic function; register |
| 9–10 | Ch 6 | Types of codes; binary oppositions; myth & ideology |
| 11–12 | Ch 7 | Actantial model; visual semiotics; branding |
| 13–14 | Ch 8 | Umwelt; biosemiotics debate; ecosemiotics mapping |
| 15 | Ch 9 | Capstone presentations |

If you teach a shorter course, the **starred core activity** in each chapter (the first one listed) is the one to keep.

## Grouping and participation

Most fieldwork and collection tasks work best as a **flipped** sequence: students gather material individually before class (a photo set, a recording, a screenshot), then analyse it in pairs or groups during the session. This guarantees everyone arrives with something concrete to contribute and keeps in-class time focused on interpretation and argument.

For large classes, use the **think–pair–share** rhythm built into many activities: a short individual attempt, a paired comparison, then a few pairs reporting to the whole class.

## A note on Thai examples and your students

The examples are grounded in Thai contexts because most early users will be based in Thailand, and concrete local material makes abstract theory legible. Two cautions:

- **Invite students' own material.** The strongest version of every activity replaces the given example with one the student chooses from their own life, region, or first language. Thai examples are a starting point, not a ceiling. Isan, Lanna, Southern, and non-Thai students should be encouraged to bring their own semiospheres.
- **Handle sensitive signs with care.** A few activities touch on religion, the monarchy, and political color symbolism. Treat these descriptively and analytically, focus on how signs work rather than on political positions, and be mindful of local legal and cultural sensitivities. Alternative examples are suggested where relevant.

## Assessment ideas

- **Formative:** collect the one-paragraph "interpretation + justification" that many activities produce. Grade lightly for use of terminology and quality of reasoning, not for a "right answer."
- **Portfolio:** have students keep a running **semiotic field journal** across the term (photos + short analyses), assembled into the [capstone](/into-semiotics/chapters/capstone/) at the end.
- **Summative:** the capstone project asks for a full multi-chapter analysis of a chosen artifact or site; a rubric is provided there.

## Adapting and contributing

All files are plain Markdown. To edit an activity, open the corresponding file in `_chapters/` and change the text; the site rebuilds automatically on GitHub Pages. To add an activity, copy an existing `<div class="activity">…</div>` block. See the `README.md` in the repository for deployment details.
